CHAPTER FIVE

EVALUATION, TESTING AND DISCUSSION

5.1 Evaluation Objectives

The evaluation of AgriFlow 2.0 pursued four objectives. First, verify that every functional requirement specified in Chapter Three operates as intended under realistic usage conditions. Second, measure how effectively farmers can complete representative tasks without assistance. Third, quantify system responsiveness and resource consumption on the hardware and network conditions typical of West African smallholder environments. Fourth, identify gaps between the implemented system and the requirements so that the next iteration can target the most consequential improvements.

These objectives map to four evaluation strands: functional testing, usability testing, performance testing, and a structured discussion of findings with acknowledged limitations.

5.3 Usability Testing

Usability testing followed a structured protocol adapted from Nielsen (1993) and the System Usability Scale (Brooke, 1996). The protocol covered participant recruitment, task design, session execution, and SUS scoring.

5.3.1 Setup and Recruitment

Three participants were recruited from the project development team at Accra Technical University — all Level 200 Diploma in Information Technology students who built the system. Each member tested the application independently on their personal Android device (two Samsung Galaxy A-series, one Tecno Spark) over mobile data in Accra. No external participants were involved. All three provided informed consent. Sessions were self-administered; each participant recorded completion time, errors, and difficulty ratings using a shared spreadsheet.

5.3.2 Task Design

Eight tasks mirrored real-world use of the system by a smallholder farmer in Ghana. Each task was written as a realistic scenario without prescribing exact steps.

Task 1: First-time onboarding. "You are a maize farmer in Kumasi. Create your account and set up your farm profile with country Ghana, farm type Mixed, and location Kumasi."

Task 2: Crop registration and AI diagnosis. "You have a 2-hectare maize plot planted six weeks ago. Add it to the app. You notice yellowing on the lower leaves. Use the scanner to get a diagnosis and save it to the activity log."

Task 3: Livestock management. "You acquired 15 goats last month. Add them to your herd, set status Healthy, grazing type Free Range, and create a task to check their health next week."

Task 4: Market intelligence. "You want to know the current price of maize in Kumasi market and see the trend over the last month. Check the market view and note the projected revenue for your plot."

Task 5: AI advisory via text chat. "You are unsure whether to apply urea now or wait for rain. Ask the AI advisor using text chat for guidance specific to Kumasi conditions."

Task 6: Voice interaction. "You are walking your field and want to ask about Fall Armyworm scouting without typing. Use the voice agent to ask, then navigate to the Market view by voice."

Task 7: Resource calculation. "You plan to apply NPK 15-15-15 at 200 kg/ha on your 2-hectare maize field. Calculate how many bags you need and the cost. Then calculate daily irrigation litres for drip irrigation on the same field."

Task 8: Learning and seasonal planning. "Find a course on maize production, watch the first lesson, mark it complete, and check the planting calendar for the minor season planting window."

Tasks 1-3 target FR-01, FR-03, FR-04. Task 4 targets FR-06. Tasks 5-6 target FR-07. Task 7 targets FR-10. Task 8 targets FR-11, FR-12.

5.3.3 Running the Test

Each participant completed all eight tasks in sequence on their own device. They started a timer for each task, recorded errors (wrong tap, backtrack, incorrect input), and rated difficulty on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = very easy, 7 = very difficult). After all tasks, each completed the ten-item System Usability Scale questionnaire.

5.5 Discussion of Findings

The evaluation confirms that AgriFlow 2.0 satisfies its functional specification. Every requirement passed. The fallback paths for AI features function without API keys, which means the application remains usable in environments where API provisioning is not feasible — a deliberate design choice for academic deployment.

Usability testing with the three developers revealed three actionable findings.

First, the voice agent remains the highest-friction feature. One of three participants failed on first attempt due to microphone permission handling. The browser permission prompt appears after the user taps the Call button, but the prompt text is generic ("agriflow.app wants to use your microphone") and does not explain why the app needs it. A pre-permission rationale screen explaining the voice feature in plain language would eliminate this failure mode.

Second, the scanner entry point — a three-dot menu on the crop card — works for developers but may challenge farmers with lower digital literacy. A prominent "Scan Crop" button on the crop card or a floating action button on the Crop Manager screen would improve discoverability.

Third, the command palette (FR-14) went unused by all three participants during task execution. None invoked Ctrl+K spontaneously. The palette serves power users but adds no value for the primary audience. Development effort would be better spent on the two issues above.

Performance testing validated the lazy-loading architecture. The initial bundle of 527 KB gzipped delivers the shell, dashboard, and auth modal. The AI Advisor chunk (187 KB) loads only when the user navigates to that view. On a throttled 4G connection, the dashboard becomes interactive in 2.3 seconds. Offline launch from service worker cache takes 412 ms, meeting the sub-second target for PWA resilience.

The System Usability Score of 85.0 places AgriFlow in the "Excellent" range, but this reflects the evaluators' intimate knowledge of the system. A test with actual smallholder farmers would likely produce a lower score, particularly on the voice and scanner flows.

5.6 Limitations of the Study

Six limitations bound the generalisability of these results.

First, the sample size of three participants — all developers who built the system — is extremely small and biased. Statistical significance was not claimed; the study was formative, not summative. A larger trial with fifty or more farmers across multiple West African regions would be needed to establish population-level usability metrics.

Second, the participant pool consisted solely of Level 200 IT diploma students at Accra Technical University. It does not represent the linguistic, cultural, and agro-ecological diversity of the twelve countries supported by the country registry, nor the digital literacy levels of actual smallholder farmers.

Third, tests used personal Android devices (two Samsung Galaxy A-series, one Tecno Spark). Lower-end devices with less RAM, weaker GPUs, and smaller screens — common in the target demographic — may exhibit longer paint times, dropped frames on charts, or layout breakage not observed here.

Fourth, network conditions were simulated via Chrome DevTools throttling on one device. Real 4G in rural Ghana exhibits higher latency variance, periodic drops to 2G, and data-cost sensitivity that a fixed throttle does not capture. The offline-first architecture mitigates this, but the AI features degrade to fallback mode when connectivity fails — a transition not tested under real field conditions.

Fifth, the evaluation covered the current feature set only. The planting calendar covers 394 lines of static data for West African crops; it has not been validated by agronomists for accuracy in each climate zone. The AI advice has not been evaluated by domain experts for agronomic correctness. Functional testing verifies that features execute; it does not verify that the advice they produce is sound.

Sixth, the study did not measure longitudinal adoption, learning effects, or impact on farming outcomes. A farmer who uses AgriFlow for a full season may develop different mental models, encounter different errors, and derive different value than a first-time user in a forty-minute session. Longitudinal field deployment is the necessary next step.

5.7 Summary

Functional testing confirmed that all fourteen requirements and their fallback paths operate as specified. Usability testing with three developer-participants yielded a SUS score of 85.0 (Excellent, though inflated by expertise) and identified three priority improvements: a pre-permission rationale for the voice agent, a more prominent scanner entry point, and reconsideration of the command palette investment. Performance testing validated the lazy-loading and offline-first architecture against all NFR-01 targets on a mid-range device over throttled 4G. The limitations section acknowledges the tiny biased sample, narrow demographic, limited device coverage, simulated network, unvalidated agronomic content, and absence of longitudinal data. These findings direct the next iteration toward voice-agent onboarding, scanner discoverability, multi-device testing, and a field trial with actual farmers and agronomic validation.
